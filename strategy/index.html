SplitHaus: website strategy and specific changes

Evidence date: September 21, 2026 · Strategy-first reading edition
Recommendation artifact only. No deployment or external action authorized.

1. Strategy: help the organizer earn family agreement

SplitHaus should sell a considered family trial, not an immediate account signup. Build the website so the person organizing the calendar can send one link, and a skeptical relative can understand the proposal, check what it means for their August weeks, assess cost and effort, and decide whether it is worth discussing.

The business objective remains adoption of a working shared-house schedule. The website’s immediate job is to help the family make the decision that precedes adoption. Do not confuse the person willing to set up software with everyone whose agreement is needed to use it. This is the working buyer model in the source material—not a measured conversion finding. [B]

The problem we are solving

Today, the captured homepage asks for setup before resolving the family’s questions. The primary CTA opens a flow with rotation and placeholder groups already selected. /how and /pricing redirect to login. A relative receiving the link is therefore being asked to do something before being helped to evaluate it. [C]

For this reader, the competing option is to keep using the existing texts, calls, paper list or shared calendar—not necessarily to buy another software product. A familiar imperfect arrangement can feel less risky than a new system that might change August allocations or create work for everyone. That is the strategic hypothesis this redesign addresses; it is not proof that scheduling dominates every family’s problems.

Make these five strategic choices

1. Sell scheduling, not a broader family-management system.

Lead with one concrete job: put the family’s chosen scheduling approach into a calendar everyone can see and follow. Keep shared costs secondary. Therefore: use a functional headline, a simple schedule example and capability-based copy; remove expense selling from the hero. Adding a second financial decision before the scheduling decision makes the proposal harder to explain and conflicts with the recorded category boundary.

2. Make evaluation safe before asking for commitment.

A skeptical relative should be able to read, inspect an example and review terms without registering or appearing to accept a new allocation policy. Therefore: make /how, /pricing and /share public; lead with “See how it works” and “Read the family summary”; acknowledge August without promising unchanged weeks. Reading about the product must not feel like starting the product.

3. Give the organizer a proposal the family can judge—not a sales pitch they must reconstruct.

The forwarded page should answer what this is, how it could handle the arrangements the family wants to keep, what it costs and what each person must do. Therefore: build a self-contained family summary with a verified example, cost and participation answers, then offer sharing. A page of questions with no answers is not enough; a share button alone does not solve the organizer’s problem.

4. Explain the mechanism before asking the reader to operate it.

An interactive calendar asks the visitor to learn controls before they know whether the idea fits. Therefore: show a short fictional schedule in readable text, explain how a supported approach produced it, and make interaction optional. Keep the founder’s first-person story as evidence of relevant experience—not as evidence that the product guarantees fairness or reliability.

5. Keep setup easy to find, but earn it.

A ready organizer should still be able to proceed. Therefore: keep a quieter setup route in navigation, place the main setup CTA after explanation, and ask how the group shares time rather than preselecting rotation. The decision journey and the product entry must tell the same story: the family evaluates and chooses an approach.

The intended journey and the job of each page

  • Homepage — understand and check relevance. What is this? What might it mean for our usual weeks? Show purpose, the August prompt, a simple example, and accessible cost/effort information.
  • /how — check whether the approach fits. Explain supported scheduling modes, how assignments happen, Match’s actual role and limitations. Do not promise universal compatibility.
  • /pricing — establish the obligation. One verified offer, billing unit and clear answers about trial, renewal, cancellation and multiple houses. Distinguish what the subscription costs from how the family divides that cost.
  • /share — support a family conversation. A complete, readable summary that stands on its own when forwarded. Let the reader review it before invoking sharing.
  • Setup — act after choosing to proceed. Ask how the group shares time; remove placeholder groups and automatic rotation assumptions.

These are jobs, not mandatory steps every visitor must complete. Let readers go directly to the answer they need.

What to do first—and what not to do

First, settle the answers: record the authoritative offer, verify scheduling/participation explanations and assign public-route ownership. Page layouts can progress in parallel, but a pricing link without an accurate price and a participation heading without an answer are unfinished work.

Then, build the decision path: implement the public pages, hero/action hierarchy, fictional proof and forwardable summary as one coherent experience. Keep copy, offer terms and setup entry consistent. Do not spend the first iteration adding another interactive demo, promoting the expense add-on, creating regional variants or adding a sticky share bar.

Before publishing, test the promises: verify public access, product behavior, mobile controls, consent and assets. Resolve the analytics-identity policy before finalizing instrumentation. These are dependencies for a credible experience, not permission to deploy.

How we will judge whether the strategy works

The immediate test is whether a consented reader can accurately explain what SplitHaus does, what happens to existing arrangements, what it costs, what they personally must do and what the next step is. The desired response is a considered “this is worth discussing,” not a mistaken belief that August weeks are guaranteed.

Use PostHog to distinguish evaluation, summary use, technical sharing outcomes and later setup/real scheduling activity. A share click is not delivery, agreement or adoption. More setup clicks alone are not success; fewer premature clicks with better understanding may be progress. There is no supported conversion target or uplift forecast yet.

Evidence strength: Astra and Terra corroborate the direction using the same source packet; this is not independent customer validation. Forty seconds is a design scenario, not measured dwell time. Section 7 preserves the source disagreements and limitations.

Existing strengths to preserve

Retain zoom to 5×, notch/keyboard-aware viewport settings, mobile-first authoring, mobile content parity, calendar ARIA labels, Dan’s first-person provenance and the billing FAQ questions. Keep “How homes stay shared.” as a brand line elsewhere, not a second hero headline. Price already appears below the current hero action; it is the fuller pricing section that sits after five objection questions. The redesign makes the answer clearer and consistent—it does not fix an absence of any price cue.

Event semantics and verification criteria

Implementation details below operationalize the source definitions; they are not deployed behavior:

  • Fire CTA events once per deliberate activation. A navigation click is not a destination view.
  • Fire public_decision_page_viewed only after successful public content loads. Allowlist the sanitized route; verify logged-out access.
  • Define and document the visibility rule before using decision_content_exposed; do not label exposure as reading or understanding.
  • For family_share_result, record only platform-observable outcomes: native API completion/cancellation/error where distinguishable, or clipboard success/failure. Do not call native API completion “sent.” Do not collect chosen recipients, message text or contacts.
  • Exclude identifiable preview-bot and synthetic-test traffic from audience results; disclose imperfect detection rather than assuming every load is human.
  • Verify emission, ingestion and aggregate receipt with non-identifying synthetic actions. Test double clicks, retries, route transitions and deduplication, success/failure paths, consent denied/granted/withdrawn, identity lifecycle, payload hygiene and counting-window rules. Disable invalid events and withdraw affected conclusions.

Funnel views—not one forced linear sequence

  1. Evaluation: landing → explanation/pricing/summary click → successful public destination → defined content exposure.
